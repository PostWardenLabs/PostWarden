# PostWarden — concepts guide

This document is the *how do I think about this* — for *why* the schema
is shaped the way it is, see [`SPEC.md`](SPEC.md); for the exact tables,
see [`SCHEMA.md`](SCHEMA.md); for how the app code is organized, see
[`ARCHITECTURE.md`](ARCHITECTURE.md). This one is for someone new to
PostWarden, possibly new to double-entry accounting entirely, who wants
to understand what they're looking at before they start typing in
numbers.

## What is PostWarden?

A personal general ledger: real double-entry bookkeeping, a proper
chart of accounts, and PostgreSQL itself — not the application sitting
on top of it — enforcing every accounting rule (balanced entries,
append-only history, a consistent account hierarchy) regardless of what
wrote the data. On top of that ledger you get a Journal for entering
transactions, Trial Balance / Income Statement / Balance Sheet reports
with a real collapsible hierarchy, a Budget grid, and **scenarios**:
your actual books plus as many what-if forecasts and budgets as you
want, all living in the same tables so comparing them is a query, not a
reconciliation project. See the root [`README.md`](https://github.com/PostWardenLabs/PostWarden#readme)'s
"What you get" for the full feature list — this guide is about the
ideas underneath those features, not a tour of the screens.

### What PostWarden isn't

- **Envelope budgeting** (Actual Budget, YNAB). There's no monthly
  ritual of assigning every dollar a job before you're allowed to
  spend it. A PostWarden budget is a scenario — the same fact table as
  your actual books, with a `scenario` dimension added — compared
  against ACTUAL by a query. See "Why double-entry for personal
  finance?" below for what that buys you.
- **A bank-synced, auto-categorized transaction feed** (Mint, Copilot,
  Monarch, or anything Plaid-backed). There's no live connection to
  your bank and nothing categorizes a transaction for you. Entries are
  typed by hand, generated by a schedule, or imported from CSV — and
  the latter two land in a Staging scenario for you to review and
  approve, never posting to your real books unsupervised.
- **Business accounting software** (QuickBooks, Xero, Wave). No
  invoicing, no vendor-facing accounts-payable workflow, no payroll, no
  sales-tax filing, no multi-entity consolidation. The chart-of-accounts
  *pattern* below borrows structure from how businesses organize
  theirs — the app itself is scoped to one person's (or household's)
  own books.
- **An investment portfolio tracker.** A brokerage or retirement
  account is a single balance you update by hand, not a computed sum of
  share lots times a live price feed. There's no cost-basis tracking,
  no ticker prices, and no realized/unrealized gain calculated for
  you — though you can post one yourself (see the 3300 account below).
- **Multi-currency**, not yet. Every account carries a `currency`
  column, but nothing in the app converts or translates between
  currencies. Pick one and stay in it until that ships — it's on
  `SPEC.md`'s Extension roadmap (a `prices` table, translated in the
  reporting views).

## What is double-entry accounting?

Every transaction touches at least two accounts, and the amounts
posted across all of them sum to zero: one or more **debits**, exactly
matched in total by one or more **credits**. Every account belongs to
one of five types — asset, liability, equity, income, expense — and
which side *increases* that account's balance is fixed by its type, not
chosen per transaction: a debit increases an asset or an expense, a
credit increases a liability, equity, or income (and the reverse
decreases them). That single rule is where the accounting equation
comes from — **Assets = Liabilities + Equity**, always true, because
every entry that changes one side of it changes the other side by
exactly the same amount.

In PostWarden this isn't a discipline you're trusting yourself to
maintain — `db/schema.sql` checks `SUM(amount) = 0` per entry as a
deferred constraint trigger at `COMMIT`, so an unbalanced entry simply
cannot be saved, no matter what client tried to write it (`SPEC.md`
decision 1). "Debit" and "credit" as separate columns are really just a
sign: `journal_lines.amount` is one signed number (positive = debit,
negative = credit), and the familiar two-column presentation is derived
from it, not the other way around.

### Why use double-entry for personal finance?

The usual objection is that double-entry is for businesses and
accountants — overkill for tracking your own money, where a spreadsheet
of transactions or an envelope app (money in, money out) is simpler. It
looks simpler right up until you need to track something that isn't
cash yet.

Say you're planning a wedding. You've picked a venue and signed a
$9,000 contract: three $3,000 installments, three months apart, each
one going on a credit card you pay off in full every month. Innocent
enough — until you try to record it. A single-entry tracker only has a
place to log money *moving*, so what do you actually type in, and
when? Log $3,000 as a "wedding" expense each time a card statement
includes an installment, and your tracker knows nothing about the
wedding until three months from now, with no field anywhere for "how
much do we still owe the venue" short of re-reading the contract. Log
the full $9,000 the day you sign instead, and now your tracker's
"spent so far" number and your checking/card balances quietly disagree
with each other, reconciled only by remembering which entry was
ceremonial and which ones were real cash leaving your account.

Double-entry has a place to put this precisely because "what you owe"
gets to be an account with a balance, not a fact you keep in your head.
Sign the contract, and post the full commitment the moment you make
it — debit Wedding Expense, credit **Accounts Payable: Venue** $9,000.
That account's balance is now "how much we still owe them," correct
from day one, no reconstruction required. Pay the first installment on
the card: debit A/P: Venue $3,000 (you owe the venue $3,000 less),
credit **Credit Card Payable** $3,000 (you owe your card issuer $3,000
more) — one obligation converts into another, and because they're
different accounts, each balance stays exactly right and the two can be
checked independently. Pay off that statement: debit Credit Card
Payable, credit Checking — the debt to the issuer clears, nothing else
moves.

At any point, "how much are we still on the hook for, between the venue
and the card" is a report, not a memory exercise. That's the actual
case for double-entry in a personal ledger: not rigor for its own sake,
but that pending, owed, and committed amounts get to *be* accounts
instead of things you reconstruct by hand every time you want to know
where you stand. (The README's own "Why double-entry for personal
finance?" section makes the same case with a different example — a
loan to a friend, and a budget kept in sync with actuals for free —
worth reading if this one didn't land.)

## Setting up the app

```bash
docker compose up --build
```

Open `http://localhost:8000` and create a login — nothing exists until
you make one. The database initializes itself on first boot: schema, a
starter chart of accounts, and three scenarios (`ACTUAL`, a `BUD2026`
budget, and a `STAGING` holding pen for imports/schedules), all from
`db/seed.sql`; `db/seed_demo.sql` adds a handful of sample entries on
top so the first screens aren't empty. See the root README's
[Quickstart](https://github.com/PostWardenLabs/PostWarden#quickstart-docker),
[Run it (local, no Docker)](https://github.com/PostWardenLabs/PostWarden#run-it-local-no-docker),
and [Creating a login](https://github.com/PostWardenLabs/PostWarden#creating-a-login)
sections for the full walkthrough, including running without Docker,
a completely blank start with no demo data, and updating later.

## I set up the app — now what?

The chart `db/seed.sql` loads is a starting point, not a prescription —
its own header comment says so. Edit it from the Accounts page
(`/accounts`) once the app is running, or edit `db/seed.sql` itself
before your first `docker compose up` if you'd rather start from a
different shape entirely. Before you do either, it helps to know the
pattern most charts of accounts — business or personal — actually
follow, and which parts of it are worth keeping.

### The business convention

Most companies lay out their chart of accounts by numeric block,
roughly:

| Range | Category |
|---|---|
| 1000–1999 | Assets |
| 2000–2999 | Liabilities |
| 3000–3999 | Equity |
| 4000–4999 | Income |
| 5000–5999 | Cost of goods sold — expenses incurred to make or sell the product |
| 6000–6999 | Operating expenses — the admin side (IT, HR, ...) |
| 7000–7999 | Financial income/expense and taxes |

Nothing in PostWarden enforces this numbering — the schema only
requires a code be 3–8 digits (`schema.sql`) and that a child account
share its parent's type — but it's worth keeping anyway, because every
accounting tool, template, and person who's ever seen a chart of
accounts recognizes a code starting with `1` as an asset without being
told.

### The parts that carry over unchanged

Assets, Liabilities, Equity, and (mostly) Income keep the business
numbering almost exactly, because the reasons a business separates
"liquid cash" from "receivables" from "fixed assets" are the same
reasons you'd want to, personally — those things behave differently,
mature at different times, and answer different questions. This is
what `db/seed.sql` actually ships:

**1000–1999: Assets**

- `1100` Liquid Cash & Cash Equivalents
  - `1110` Checking
  - `1120` Savings
  - `1130` Physical Cash
- `1200` Short-Term Receivables — money owed *to* you (reimbursements
  pending, an IOU from a friend) — the account that makes the wedding
  example above possible in reverse
  - `1210` Money Owed by Friends/Family
- `1300` Investment Assets
  - `1310` Brokerage
  - `1320` Retirement (401(k), IRA, pension)
- `1400` Fixed & Illiquid Assets
  - `1410` Vehicles

**2000–2999: Liabilities**

- `2100` Current Liabilities
  - `2110` Credit Cards
- `2200` Long-Term Liabilities
  - `2210` Mortgage
  - `2220` Loans (auto, student, personal)

**3000–3999: Equity**

- `3100` Opening Balances — where your net worth starts when you first
  set up the books
- `3200` Retained Earnings — prior years' accumulated savings
- `3300` Unrealized Gains/Losses — a place to post the change in market
  value of something like a brokerage balance or a home, without it
  looking like income or an expense

**4000–4999: Income** — `4100` Salary, `4200` Interest & Dividend
Income, `4300` Other Income. Kept deliberately shallow; see the next
section for why one top-level account is usually enough.

Not everything from a fuller version of this pattern made it into the
starter file — it stays lean on purpose. Worth adding if it applies to
you: a Crypto/Alternative Assets leaf under `1300`, a Primary Residence
or High-Value Personal Property leaf under `1400`, a second receivables
leaf for reimbursable work expenses specifically, or splitting `2220`
into separate Auto/Student/Personal Loan accounts once tracking them
apart actually matters to you.

### Should you have more than one Income top-level account?

Usually not. One `4000` Income account with a subaccount per source
(salary, interest, side income, ...) is enough for almost everyone,
because the whole point of consolidating everything into one Income
Statement is to see it as one number. Where a second top-level income
account earns its keep is when you're running something that deserves
its *own* profit-and-loss view, separable from your personal one — a
side business, a rental property, a freelance practice you actually
want to report on in isolation before it rolls up into your household
totals. That's the same instinct behind `SPEC.md`'s "entity dimension"
roadmap item (an `entities` table and `entity_id` on entries, so one
fact table can consolidate multiple sets of books) — not shipped yet,
but you can approximate it today with a second top-level `4xxx`/`5xxx`
pair for that activity plus a tag to filter by it.

### Structuring expenses: two philosophies

Assets, Liabilities, Equity, and Income are the parts every pattern
above already agrees on. Expenses are where personal charts diverge —
both from the business convention and from each other — because "how
do I separate the cost of goods sold from SG&A" isn't a distinction
that helps you budget, save, or track your own spending. The two
patterns below organize expenses instead around questions people
actually ask about their own money. Pick one, mix pieces of both, or
write your own — the numbering is yours to keep or break.

#### Option 1: Needs vs. wants (what `db/seed.sql` ships)

Splits every expense by how negotiable it is, and keeps taxes and debt
service visible as their own thing instead of buried inside "spending."

- **`5000` Fixed & Essential Living (Needs)** — non-negotiable, hard to
  reduce short-term: `5100` Housing & Utilities (`5110` Rent/Mortgage,
  `5120` Utilities), `5200` Transportation, `5300` Health & Insurance
  (`5310` Insurance, `5320` Medical & Pharmacy), `5400` Groceries.
- **`6000` Flexible & Lifestyle (Wants)** — the first thing you cut
  when tightening up: `6100` Dining Out & Coffee, `6200` Entertainment
  & Subscriptions, `6300` Shopping & Personal Care, `6400` Travel &
  Vacations.
- **`7000` Taxes** — mandatory statutory deductions, isolated so
  "actual spendable income" is a real number instead of taxes buried
  inside a paycheck's net figure: `7100` Income Tax, `7200` Payroll
  Taxes.
- **`8000` Debt, Financial & Professional Costs** — the cost of
  servicing debt and managing money, not the debt itself (that's a
  liability): `8100` Interest (credit card, loans), `8200` Banking &
  Investment Fees, `8300` Professional & Career Development.
- **`9000` Gifts, Losses & Exceptional Items** — irregular or
  altruistic outflows that would otherwise distort a monthly baseline:
  `9100` Charitable Giving, `9200` Gifts to Family/Friends, `9300`
  Extraordinary/One-off Losses.

Notice `5400` Groceries and `6100` Dining Out are deliberately *not*
under one "Food" account the way a simpler chart might lump them — that
split is the whole point of this pattern: two purchases that look
identical on a receipt (money spent on food) land in different buckets
because one is closer to non-negotiable and the other isn't.

Worth splitting out further as your chart grows: capital gains tax
under `7000` if you have realized investment gains, foreign-exchange or
transfer fees under `8200`, political/community giving separated from
charitable donations under `9100`, uninsured casualty or theft losses
under `9300`.

#### Option 2: Savings rate first

Strictly, moving cash into a 401(k) or a brokerage account is an
asset-to-asset transfer (`1110` Cash → `1310` Brokerage) — it never
touches an income-statement account at all. This pattern deliberately
breaks that purity: it adds a `5000` **Capital Accumulation & Wealth
Transfers** block (`5100` Retirement/IRA/Brokerage contributions,
`5200` sinking funds — emergency, home/car replacement, future travel)
so your Income Statement shows a savings rate as a literal line, above
everything else, before any lifestyle spending is subtracted. Living
costs (`6000`), taxes (`7000`), and the rest of lifestyle spending
(`8000`–`9000`) follow after, organized by how you experience them
rather than by need vs. want.

The trade-off is real: this makes "expense" mean something slightly
different than the strict double-entry definition (money leaving the
household for good) in exchange for a P&L that visually foregrounds one
specific metric — how much you're setting aside before you look at
anything else. It suits someone deliberately optimizing a savings rate
as the headline number (the FIRE-community style of tracking); Option 1
suits someone who'd rather keep "expense" meaning what it strictly
means and see savings progress through the Budget grid or a scenario
comparison instead.

### Picking one

Option 1 is the default for a reason: it doesn't ask you to redefine
what "expense" means, it's the pattern `db/seed.sql` already ships, and
Needs vs. Wants is a distinction most people already think in even
before they've ever seen a chart of accounts. Reach for Option 2
specifically if maximizing and watching a savings rate *is* the
question you're trying to answer — otherwise it's extra conceptual
overhead for a view the Budget grid and scenario comparisons already
give you for free. Either way, nothing about the choice is permanent:
the chart lives in an ordinary Postgres table, editable from the
Accounts page at any time, and `db/seed.sql`'s own opening comment
means exactly what it says — this is a starting point, not a
prescription.
